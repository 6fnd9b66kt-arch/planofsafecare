Plans of Safe Care: State-by-State Summary

How each state and the District of Columbia carries out the federal Plan of Safe Care requirement for infants with prenatal substance exposure and their families. From planofsafecare.org, maintained by Margaret Lloyd Sieger, PhD, MS, University of Kansas School of Medicine. Generated November 9, 2026.

Download PDFDownload spreadsheetBack to the interactive map

State policies change. If you know of an update for your state, please email msieger@kumc.edu.

Approach categories

  • Hospital-initiated. Health care or community providers develop the plan during pregnancy or soon after birth, outside the child welfare system and before any decision about a report.
  • Dual Pathways. Some exposures, such as medication for opioid use disorder, lead to a hospital-initiated plan. Other exposures require a report and a child protective services response.
  • All CPS Referrals. Every screened-in report for prenatal substance exposure gets a plan, developed during the child protective services assessment whether or not a case is opened.
  • Alternative Response. Plans are delivered through a voluntary, in-home child welfare track, without a formal investigation or court involvement.
  • Open Child Welfare Case. Plans are developed only after a family is found to need child welfare intervention. The plan works as a safety plan or case plan.
  • No Policy. No statewide Plan of Safe Care policy was identified as of 2024.

Categories reflect state policy as of 2024 (Lloyd Sieger et al., 2026), except Michigan, Pennsylvania, Virginia, and Washington, which have been updated since. Bracketed numbers refer to the sources at the end.

Alabama

Profile last updated: October 2026

All CPS Referrals[1]. Every screened-in report for prenatal substance exposure gets a plan, developed during the child protective services assessment whether or not a case is opened.

How it works in practice[1]

Hospitals contact the Department of Human Resources whenever a doctor determines a newborn was affected by substance use or withdrawal. The plan is not a separate document. Caseworkers build it into the family’s safety plan or case plan during the investigation or when a case is opened for services.

Profile last updated: October 2026

All CPS Referrals[1]. Every screened-in report for prenatal substance exposure gets a plan, developed during the child protective services assessment whether or not a case is opened.

How it works in practice[8]

Colorado encourages starting the plan during pregnancy or at the birth hospital before discharge, with the parent and a team that can include health care, treatment, and peer support providers. Some hospitals send the state a notification without names, which is not a report to child welfare. A report is required only when there are safety concerns, and if a screened-in report has no plan, a caseworker creates one.

Connecticut

Profile last updated: October 2026

Hospital-initiated[1]. Health care or community providers develop the plan during pregnancy or soon after birth, outside the child welfare system and before any decision about a report.

How it works in practice[6]

Hospitals use an online portal to decide whether there is a safety concern. If there is none, they send an anonymous notification with no identifying information about the family. If there is a safety concern, they make a formal report to child welfare, which leads to an investigation. At the time of the study, Connecticut was reconsidering whether a positive infant drug test should be a reason to report.

Delaware

Profile last updated: October 2026

Alternative Response[1]. Plans are delivered through a voluntary, in-home child welfare track, without a formal investigation or court involvement.

How it works in practice[1]

Hospitals notify the Division of Family Services about substance-exposed infants and infants with fetal alcohol spectrum disorder, and a plan is developed before the baby leaves the hospital. Delaware law says prenatal exposure alone is not abuse or neglect, but it leads to an investigation or family assessment. When a mother is taking a prescribed medication, hospitals can use a Medical Plan of Safe Care that the medical provider carries out and monitors.

Indiana

Profile last updated: November 2026

All CPS Referrals[1]. Every screened-in report for prenatal substance exposure gets a plan, developed during the child protective services assessment whether or not a case is opened.

How it works in practice[1][9]

A plan must be developed for every infant under age 1 identified as affected by legal or illegal substances, withdrawal, neonatal abstinence syndrome, or fetal alcohol spectrum disorder. It covers the health and treatment needs of the infant, parents, household members, and caregivers, and is developed whether or not the assessment is substantiated. If the case is opened and substantiated, or the family receives later reports, the plan must be updated until the child turns 1. The plan is also sent to the child’s primary care provider.

Louisiana

Profile last updated: October 2026

Dual Pathways[1]. Some exposures, such as medication for opioid use disorder, lead to a hospital-initiated plan. Other exposures require a report and a child protective services response.

How it works in practice[1]

When a newborn shows withdrawal or other effects from the mother’s lawful use of a prescribed medication, the physician fills out a notification form that states no prenatal neglect is suspected, instead of calling the child abuse hotline. Other exposures go through the regular child protection response. The state tracks whether a plan was developed and referrals were made before discharge.

Profile last updated: November 2026

All CPS Referrals[1]. Every screened-in report for prenatal substance exposure gets a plan, developed during the child protective services assessment whether or not a case is opened.

Updated since the 2024 policy scan. Professionals in Michigan report that a plan is required for every substance-exposed infant referred to child protective services, so its approach is now shown as All CPS Referrals.[9]

A plan is required for every substance-exposed infant referred to child protective services, including infants exposed to THC (marijuana). Caseworkers develop the plan. Michigan’s protocol covers three points in time, during pregnancy, at birth, and after birth, and is shared with child welfare staff, medical professionals, and service providers.

New Mexico

Hospital-initiated[1]. Health care or community providers develop the plan during pregnancy or soon after birth, outside the child welfare system and before any decision about a report.

New Mexico substantially changed its mandated reporting requirements in 2025. The reporting details below may not reflect those changes. Its approach is still hospital-initiated.

How it works in practice[6]

Hospitals send either a notification or a report, as in Connecticut, but notifications include the family’s identifying information. Plan information is kept separate from the main child welfare data system. Medicaid managed care organizations coordinate care after the hospital stay and receive plans automatically through the state portal, and a navigator helps connect child welfare staff with care coordinators. State law (House Bill 230, 2019) gives managed care organizations this role. In 2025, the state issued a directive requiring the child welfare agency to seek custody of certain substance-exposed newborns.
